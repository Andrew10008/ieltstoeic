## Song 11: "Who, Which, That, and Whose"
**Target Challenge 61: Relative Pronoun Confusion**

**Lexical Chunks:**
- **the employee who** (person - subject)
- **the employee whom** (person - object)
- **the report which/that** (thing)
- **the manager whose** (possession)
- **the company that** (organization)
- **people who** (persons plural)
- **documents that** (things plural)

---

**[Verse 1]**
**The employee who** submitted the proposal has been promoted,
**who** for people as the subject, that's devoted,
**the employee whom** we hired last month is very skilled,
**whom** for people as the object, properly filled

**[Chorus]**
**Who**, **which**, **that**, and **whose**—
**who** for people doing, **which** for things you choose,
**that** works for both, but **which** adds extra info through,
**whose** shows possession—whose idea? whose plan? whose view?

**[Verse 2]**
**The report which** was submitted yesterday needs revision,
**which** or **that** for things, make your decision,
**the report that** contains the data is on your desk,
**that** is more common in speech, pass the test

**[Bridge - Whose for Possession]**
**The manager whose** office is on the third floor,
**whose** shows belonging, connected to what came before,
**the company whose** profits increased will expand,
**whose** links possession to the preceding brand

**[Verse 3 - Rules and Distinctions]**
**People who** work here receive excellent benefits,
**documents that** require signatures are in the files, that's it,
non-defining clauses use **which**, not **that**, with commas clear:
"The budget, **which** was approved last week, will take effect here"

**[Final Chorus]**
**Who** for people, **which** for things,
**that** for both—flexibility it brings,
**whom** for objects (formal speech),
**whose** for ownership within your reach!

## Song 12: "To Do or Doing?"
**Target Challenge 62: Infinitive vs. Gerund Errors**

**Lexical Chunks:**
- **want to go** (infinitive)
- **enjoy going** (gerund)
- **decide to attend** (infinitive)
- **finish working** (gerund)
- **plan to expand** (infinitive)
- **avoid making** (gerund)
- **need to complete** (infinitive)
- **suggest reviewing** (gerund)

---

**[Verse 1]**
We **want to go** to the conference in October,
infinitive after "want," the choice is sober,
they **enjoy going** to team-building events each year,
gerund after "enjoy," that's very clear

**[Chorus]**
To do or doing? Check the verb before,
some take infinitive, some take gerund—that's the core,
**decide to attend**, **finish working** on the task,
patterns you can memorize, that's all I ask

**[Verse 2]**
The company will **plan to expand** into new markets soon,
**plan** takes infinitive, singing the right tune,
we should **avoid making** the same mistake twice,
**avoid** takes gerund, that's the advice

**[Bridge - Verb Lists]**
**Want**, **decide**, **plan**, **need**, **agree**, **hope**, **expect**—
infinitives follow, show respect,
**enjoy**, **finish**, **avoid**, **suggest**, **recommend**, **practice**, **mind**—
gerunds come after, keep that in mind

**[Verse 3 - Some Take Both]**
**Begin**, **start**, **continue**, **prefer**—they take both forms,
**I started to work** / **I started working**—both are norms,
but **stop** changes meaning with what follows through:
"**stopped to smoke**" (paused for a smoke) vs. "**stopped smoking**" (quit it, true!)

**[Final Chorus]**
To do or doing? Learn the verb that came before,
**need to complete**, **finish working**—memorize the score,
infinitive shows purpose, potential, or intent,
gerund shows the action as a noun event!

**[Outro - Memory Trick]**
If the first verb expresses wishes, plans, decisions made,
infinitive follows—"**to**" is the blade,
if it's about experiences, feelings, completion,
gerund comes after—"-ing" in repetition!

## Song 13: "It Was Done, It Is Being Done"
**Target Challenge 63: Passive Voice Recognition**

**Lexical Chunks:**
- **is completed** (present passive)
- **was completed** (past passive)
- **will be completed** (future passive)
- **has been completed** (present perfect passive)
- **is being completed** (present continuous passive)
- **the report was written by** (agent)
- **the meeting is scheduled for** (passive without agent)

---

**[Verse 1]**
**The report is completed** every Friday afternoon,
present passive voice, like a regular tune,
**the report was completed** ahead of schedule last week,
past passive shows it's done, that's what we speak

**[Chorus]**
It was done, it is being done, it will be done,
passive voice shifts focus—the action, not who begun,
**be** + past participle is the pattern we use,
when the doer's unknown or doesn't matter—you choose

**[Verse 2]**
**The project will be completed** by the end of the month,
future passive structure—that's the truth upfront,
**the proposal has been completed** and submitted today,
present perfect passive shows recent completion in play

**[Bridge - Being]**
**The document is being completed** as we speak,
present continuous passive, action at its peak,
**was being reviewed** when the power went out,
past continuous passive shows what it's about

**[Verse 3 - With and Without Agent]**
**The report was written by** the marketing team,
"by" introduces the agent—who did it, supreme,
**the meeting is scheduled for** next Tuesday at two,
no agent needed when it's not important who

**[Final Chorus]**
Passive when the action matters more than who,
**forms are submitted**, **decisions are made**—it's true,
**be** in the right tense + past participle form,
passive voice construction—that's the norm!

**[Outro - Active vs. Passive]**
Active: "The manager approved the budget"
Passive: "The budget was approved"
Active: "We will complete the report"
Passive: "The report will be completed"
Focus shifts—that's the move!

## Song 14: "If This, Then That"
**Target Challenge 64: Conditional Structure Mistakes**

**Lexical Chunks:**
- **if we receive** (present), **we will process** (future) - First Conditional
- **if we received** (past), **we would process** (would + base) - Second Conditional
- **if we had received** (past perfect), **we would have processed** (would have + PP) - Third Conditional
- **if the weather is** (present), **we go** (present) - Zero Conditional

---

**[Verse 1 - First Conditional]**
**If we receive** the payment today, **we will process** your order,
first conditional: real future, crossing that border,
present tense in "if" clause, "will" in the result,
this could really happen—that's the default

**[Chorus]**
If this, then that—conditionals show the way,
real or imaginary, present or yesterday,
**if** + tense combination, **then** the consequence clear,
match the pattern to the meaning you want to steer

**[Verse 2 - Second Conditional]**
**If we received** more funding, **we would expand** the team,
second conditional: imaginary scheme,
past tense in "if" clause (but present meaning implied),
"would" in result clause—hypothetical guide

**[Verse 3 - Third Conditional]**
**If we had received** the data earlier, **we would have completed** the report,
third conditional: past that didn't happen, that sort,
past perfect in "if," "would have" + past participle too,
regret, impossible past—retrospective view

**[Bridge - Zero Conditional]**
**If the weather is** good, **we go** for a walk at lunch,
zero conditional: general truth, that's my hunch,
present in both clauses, always happens this way,
facts and habits—come what may

**[Final Chorus - All Together]**
**If** + present, **will** + base = likely future (First),
**If** + past, **would** + base = imaginary thirst (Second),
**If** + past perfect, **would have** + PP = past unreal (Third),
**If** + present, present = general truth, the word!

**[Outro - Common Mistakes]**
Don't say: "If I **will** receive..."—wrong in the "if" part!
Don't say: "If I **would have** known..."—that's not smart!
"If I **had** known" is correct for the past,
conditional structures—get them right at last!

## Song 15: "Better, Best, More, and Most"
**Target Challenge 65: Comparative/Superlative Errors**

**Lexical Chunks:**
- **cheaper than** (comparative -er)
- **the cheapest** (superlative -est)
- **more expensive than** (comparative more)
- **the most expensive** (superlative most)
- **better than** (irregular)
- **the best** (irregular)
- **as expensive as** (equality)
- **less expensive than** (less comparison)

---

**[Verse 1]**
This option is **cheaper than** the alternative we reviewed,
short adjectives add **-er** for comparing two,
this is **the cheapest** option of all three proposals here,
**-est** for superlative when one's supreme and clear

**[Chorus]**
Better, best, more, and most,
comparing two or choosing what deserves the toast,
**-er** and **-est** for short words, one or two syllables long,
**more** and **most** for longer words—that's the song!

**[Verse 2]**
This model is **more expensive than** the previous version,
longer adjectives use **more** for comparison immersion,
this is **the most expensive** product in our entire line,
**most** before the adjective when one stands above the design

**[Bridge - Irregular Forms]**
**Good** becomes **better** (comparative form),
**the best** (superlative)—that's the norm,
**bad** becomes **worse**, **the worst** of the pack,
**far** becomes **farther/further**, keep track!

**[Verse 3 - Equality and Less]**
This apartment is **as expensive as** the one downtown,
**as... as** shows they're equal, same price renown,
this laptop is **less expensive than** the Mac we saw,
**less** for lower degree, that's the law

**[Final Chorus - Rules Summary]**
One syllable: add **-er** / **-est** (cheap, cheaper, cheapest),
two syllables ending in -y: change to **-ier** / **-iest** (happy, happier, happiest),
longer words: use **more** / **most** (efficient, more efficient, most efficient),
irregulars memorize: **good/better/best**, resilient!

**[Outro - Common Mistakes to Avoid]**
Don't say "**more better**"—that's doubling, wrong!
Don't say "**most cheapest**"—doesn't belong!
Choose **-er/-est** OR **more/most**, not both in play,
comparatives and superlatives—the correct way!
